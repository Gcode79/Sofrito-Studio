5 signs your food brand reads as commodity

SP-01

Pillar: Brand clarity · Offer: Sofrito Session · CTA: sofritostudio.com/session · Carousel 7 frames · IG feed + FB repurpose

Cover
Good food isn’t the differentiator you think it is.
Sign 1
Your name could be swapped.

If your name works equally for 30 other spots on the same street, it’s working for none of them. A commodity name is interchangeable. A brand name gets read, remembered, and repeated.

Sign 2
Your menu is a list, not a pitch.

Commodity menus recite ingredients. Brand menus sell an experience. Same plate, different persuasion — the second one prices higher and parts with fewer discounts.

Sign 3
Your website can’t answer “why you.”

Three seconds on a phone is all you get. If the first screen doesn’t say who you are, what you do, and why you’re the one — the buyer assumes the others are the same.

Sign 4
Your story is “authentic and family.”

Authenticity is the minimum, not the differentiator. The brands people pick have a specific point of view — a flavor philosophy, a place, a standard — not a generic promise.

Sign 5
You market price and photos, not identity.

Discounts and pretty plates are what commoditized brands compete on. Identity is the only thing a competitor can’t copy.

Close
None of these is a death sentence. All are solvable — and you don’t need a full rebrand to start.

Your website has three seconds on a phone

SP-02

Pillar: Website & conversion · Offer: Sofrito Session → Plato · CTA: sofritostudio.com/session · Carousel 6 frames · IG feed + FB repurpose

Cover
Your website has three seconds on a phone. It’s showing the wrong screens.
Screen 1 · The point
Who are you, what do you make, where are you.

Before appetite, before photos — the first screen must answer three questions. If a buyer can’t answer them in the time it takes to blink, they’re gone.

Screen 2 · The proof
Show, not tell: the plate, the people, the place.

This is the screen that does your selling — and the one most owners get wrong by defaulting to a generic hero image.

Screen 3 · The action
Order, call, directions, book, cater — two taps away.

The third screen decides everything. If the action isn’t two taps away, the site is a brochure with a lock on the door.

What most food sites show
Same three screens, wrong content.

An intro paragraph nobody reads … a stock photo of a general vibe … and a contact form at the bottom of a scroll nobody finishes.

Your menu is a sales page

SP-03

Pillar: Website & conversion · Offer: Sofrito Session (menu review) · CTA: save + sofritostudio.com/session · Carousel 7 frames · IG feed + FB repurpose

Cover
You redesigned everything except the one thing every guest reads: the menu.
Menus are read like pages
Guests scan top-to-bottom, left-to-right, like a headline.

The F-pattern from print design applies on paper and on screen. Whatever you want people to notice has to live where the eye lands first.

Every menu needs a hero
One anchor dish — best margin, best story, most defining.

Placed where the eye lands first, it sets the reference for every other price. No hero, and guests default to the cheapest or the first.

Anchoring is free profit
A confident mid-price item makes your real target feel reasonable.

This isn’t manipulation of taste — it’s the same page-design the big chains pay agencies for.

Descriptions sell, adjectives don’t
“Bathed in butter” stops a scan. “Buttery” gets skipped.

Write the food the way the kitchen’s proudest regular describes it after two visits.

The menu is a mirror of the brand
If yours reads like every other same-cuisine menu, guests feel it.

The menu is the cheapest rebrand you’ll ever buy.
